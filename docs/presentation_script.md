Slide 1: Economic narratives & consumer sentiment
This project asks why consumer sentiment can appear weak while headline economic indicators look stronger. The setting is the months before the November 2026 midterm elections, with an information cutoff of October 13. I compare three explanations: pressures that headline indicators miss, selective information or framing, and differences in measurement or timing. The aim is to separate these explanations with evidence. A negative story is not automatically biased, and a pessimistic consumer is not automatically irrational. The practical application is better forecasting and risk monitoring, rather than a presumed media effect.

Slide 2: The input audit changes the analysis
Reading the supplied workbook first exposed three important timing and definition problems. Michigan released the October preliminary sentiment reading of 46.3 on October 9, so that observation is usable despite its month-end label. The Q3 GDP value cannot be used because the official advance release is scheduled for October 29. The August PCE figure in the workbook also differs from official headline inflation. I therefore compute inflation from the headline price index and use historical ALFRED snapshots for the economic predictors. These corrections matter because otherwise later information can appear to explain earlier perceptions.

Slide 3: Define the gap with an honest forecast
The economic model uses unemployment, headline year-over-year PCE inflation and the latest published quarterly GDP growth. Each prediction is evaluated chronologically. I define the gap as observed sentiment minus the economic prediction, and I report uncertainty and coverage. A negative residual means that this model predicted more optimism than the survey measured. It does not identify bias. The chart also shows why structural change matters: relationships estimated in earlier years may not describe the pandemic, the inflation surge or the current period. Persistence and household-control models provide important alternatives to the basic three-variable model.

Slide 4: Prices and household experience remain plausible
There are good reasons for household experience to differ from national aggregates. Falling inflation means prices are rising more slowly; it does not undo earlier price increases. Real GDP growth says little about a particular household’s housing costs, debt payments or job insecurity. I include real disposable income growth and cumulative prices as manageable alternatives, while acknowledging remaining omissions. Michigan also changed survey collection methods and documents demographic and partisan differences. These considerations can explain part of an apparent gap without any distorted media coverage. A national aggregate should not be presented as the experience of every household.

Slide 5: What the media data can measure
The media sample uses the same broad economic queries across a fixed national and local outlet panel, plus user-posted story titles on Hacker News. I average within outlets and groups, and show missing sources and sample sizes. The unit is a title, so I do not claim to have measured full arguments or audience exposure. A pinned contextual model and a dictionary provide tone measures. Transparent rules provide salience and framing proxies. A numerical token only identifies a claim worth checking. Without matching the metric, period and available official release, it cannot establish whether a headline is factually wrong.

Slide 6: Prediction: compare the same months
On the common 44-month chronological assessment sample, adding the specified media variables reduces RMSE by 0.01 index points relative to M1. With lagged sentiment included, media reduces RMSE by 0.04 points. These comparisons use common assessment months so missing media observations do not create an unfair advantage. I also include lagged-sentiment models because persistence is a strong benchmark. The analysis reports forecast errors, interval coverage, coefficient paths and sensitivity checks. Media variables can help predict sentiment by capturing genuine household pressures or shared shocks, even if the coverage is entirely accurate. A lower error therefore supports forecasting usefulness under these measurements, but does not by itself support a media-bias explanation or a causal effect.

Slide 7: Validation is a material limitation
The final assignment requires at least 300 items independently labeled by two humans. Those human labels are not available. I prepared blinded worksheets, a labeling guide, an adjudication workflow and validation code, and keep any independent AI review clearly identified as AI. It would be misleading to report AI agreement as human agreement or to call dictionary agreement accuracy. Until the human task is actually completed, the media measurements remain provisional. This limitation is displayed in the report and dashboard because it directly affects how much confidence a forecasting team should place in the narrative indicators.

Slide 8: Causal impact requires stronger identification
The chosen causal route is observational analysis with a stronger-design proposal. Pessimistic consumers may seek negative content, publishers may respond to demand, and economic shocks can drive both coverage and sentiment. A feasible experiment would randomly assign equally accurate price-level or inflation-rate framings of the same official information. It would measure baseline beliefs, subsequent perceptions and recall, with pre-registered contrasts and appropriate approval. Even a well-identified effect in that sample would not establish an effect on the national Michigan index. That would also require population exposure estimates, representative weighting and defensible transportability assumptions.

Slide 9: Practical implication: monitor, validate, then act
The useful deliverable is an updateable monitoring process. The dashboard shows the economic forecast, uncertainty, the gap, provisional narrative measures, source coverage and upcoming releases. A team can use it to ask whether the model is missing household pressure or whether a narrative change merits closer research. It should preserve snapshots and check prospective performance before changing investment exposure. The study does not estimate a profitable trading strategy or a causal national media effect. Its main conclusion is that timing, definitions, validation and alternative explanations have to be resolved before an apparent sentiment discrepancy becomes a financial signal.
