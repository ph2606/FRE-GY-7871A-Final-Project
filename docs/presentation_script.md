Slide 1: The economy and how people feel
People can hear that the economy is growing and still struggle with their bills. This project asks why. Consumer sentiment means people’s outlook for their finances and the economy. Michigan measures it with a survey score. Higher means more optimism, but it is not a percentage or a scale capped at one hundred. We compare three possibilities: pressures national statistics miss, the stories people see, and problems with measurement. Artificial intelligence, or AI, labels headline wording. It does not tell us what readers believe. The economic study stops at October 13, before the November election.

Slide 2: Use only what was known then
Imagine answering a quiz after seeing the answer sheet. A forecast has the same problem if it uses information published later. We therefore check each release date and recover earlier published economic data. The October survey score of 46.3 was first released October 9, so it is available for monitoring. But July-to-September output was not due until October 29, after the cutoff. The spreadsheet’s inflation series also leaves out food and energy, while this assignment requires the broader measure. Jobs, prices and output must all be measured consistently before we ask whether the news explains anything.

Slide 3: The gap is a forecast mistake
The first model, M1, learns how unemployment, prices and output related to earlier surveys. Then it predicts a later month. The gap means actual minus predicted. If actual sentiment is sixty and the forecast is seventy, the gap is minus ten. That means the model expected too much optimism. It does not prove that people are irrational or that the media misled them. The shaded band shows a plausible range, based on earlier forecast mistakes. The model often misses that range, which warns that it leaves important information out. Simply using last month’s survey is a strong comparison.

Slide 4: Slower inflation still leaves high prices
Suppose a shopping basket costs one hundred dollars, then one hundred ten, then one hundred twelve dollars and twenty cents. Inflation slows from ten percent to two percent, but the basket still costs much more than before. That is why the study adds accumulated prices and income after inflation. National output also misses a household’s rent, debts and job worries. Michigan changed from phone interviews to web collection during 2024. Allowing for that change shrinks the model’s gap, but many things changed together, so it cannot prove the survey method caused it. These alternatives remain plausible without assuming distorted news.

Slide 5: A headline sample is not an audience
The sample combines national and local news with titles shared on Hacker News, a technology community. The same broad searches cover jobs, prices, wages, housing and the economy. They do not search only for negative stories. Each source and source group receives balanced weight, so a large publisher cannot win by supplying more headlines. Software estimates positive or negative wording, and simple word rules flag topics and frames. But a calmly worded report of job losses can be both negative and true. Titles can change or disappear, and we do not know which ones survey respondents actually saw.

Slide 6: Provider bars compare wording, not truth
These bars compare providers covering the same official reports. Suppose forty percent of one provider’s headlines are called negative, while the others average thirty percent. Forty minus thirty is ten percentage points, so its bar is plus ten. Each report gets equal weight. Zero means matching these sampled peers, not being perfectly neutral. The lines show how the result varies when we repeatedly sample the observed months. They leave out missing-headline and AI-label errors. Providers can truthfully emphasize different details, such as wages or unemployment. There are only six shared reports and no human validation for this provider sample. The bars do not measure dishonesty, political loyalty or effects on readers.

Slide 7: Do headlines improve the forecast?
We compare forecasts using exactly the same test months. The error score is called root mean squared error, or RMSE. Square each mistake, average those squares, then take the square root. This gives large mistakes extra weight. Lower is better. Across the same 44 test months, adding headline tone lowers the forecast-error score by 0.01 survey points. When the model also knows last month’s survey, headlines lower the score by 0.04 points. These are very small gains, and statistical checks do not establish a reliable improvement. Models that know the previous survey do much better than the basic economic model. Even a useful headline signal could reflect real household pressures or shared shocks; it would not prove that news caused sentiment to fall.

Slide 8: Two human reviewers are still missing
A label is a judgment such as negative, neutral or positive. The assignment requires two people to label at least three hundred items independently. Those completed human labels are still missing. We prepared different-order worksheets, hidden source information and an evidence guide. Two AI reviewers agreed on ninety-two percent of the original three hundred titles, but machines can share the same mistake. Their agreement is not accuracy checked against people. Later relevance checks retain two hundred seventy-seven of those titles. The notebook separates results by source and label, and the report clearly identifies this unfinished requirement.

Slide 9: How could wording change beliefs?
Two things moving together does not prove cause and effect. Bad economic news can lower both headline tone and sentiment. Worried people may also seek bad news. A stronger test would randomly assign consenting volunteers different truthful summaries of the same report, perhaps high prices versus slowing inflation. Keep the facts and length the same and compare beliefs before and after. Set the plan and required sample size in advance, and obtain approval before recruiting. No participants were recruited here. Even an effect in volunteers would require evidence about the wider population’s exposure and representativeness before explaining the national survey.

Slide 10: Three trading ideas: one news signal
The news gap is national minus local negative wording, not the survey gap. Compare last month’s score with the middle of the previous twelve scores. The screen shows three fixed ideas: fade unusual negativity, follow it, or wait for it to turn. UPRO targets three times a broad stock index’s daily rise; SPXU targets the opposite. BIL holds government bills; SPY is an ordinary stock fund. Daily reset means three times daily is not three times the whole month.

Slide 11: More risk did not produce better growth
Starting with ten thousand dollars, Fade finishes near 14,352; Follow near 1,852; Wait near 13,868. Ordinary stocks finish near 21,734. The news rules suffer peak-to-later falls of about sixty-four, eighty-nine and thirty-four percent. A fall is measured from an earlier high, not the initial balance. Two rules made money, but all three grew less and fell more than ordinary stocks. Costs are one dollar per thousand bought or sold, including entry and exit. The headlines and model were reconstructed later, and human checks remain missing. Nothing here proves future profit.
